Build a Sleeper
Planter This
Weekend

Railway sleepers are one of the most honest materials you can work with. Dense, pre-weathered by decades of use, beautiful in a way that no factory-finish timber can match. A sleeper planter built properly will outlast you, your children, and quite possibly your grandchildren.

  1. 1Choose your sleepers. Opt for untreated oak or reclaimed softwood for food growing — avoid old creosote-treated sleepers near edibles. Two sleepers deep gives you 450 mm of growing depth.
  2. 2Mark out your bed. A classic working size: 1.2 m × 2.4 m. Wide enough to reach the centre from both sides without stepping on the soil.
  3. 3Stack in log-cabin style, alternating end joints for structural strength. Drill vertically through each layer and pin with 600 mm rebar spikes — no screws, no brackets, just steel and gravity.
  4. 4Line the inside with landscape fabric, not plastic. Fabric retains soil while letting roots breathe and drainage flow.
  5. 5Fill in layers: rubble at the base, then compost, topped with a 50/50 mix of topsoil and compost. Expect it to settle 80–100 mm in the first month.
  6. 6Wait one week, then plant. Your sleeper planter reaches its best in year three, as the wood greys beautifully and the soil biology matures.

What You’ll Need

  • 4–6 untreated oak sleepers (2.4 m × 200 mm × 100 mm)
  • 4 × 600 mm rebar spikes + hammer drill, 20 mm bit
  • Landscape fabric + heavy-duty staple gun
  • 1 tonne mixed topsoil & compost blend
  • Wheelbarrow, half a Saturday, a willing friend
  • Strong tea (essential)
Sourcing in Frome: The Reclamation Yard on Wallbridge often carries reclaimed railway sleepers. Ask for sustainably sourced oak — it costs more than imported pine but lasts forty years. Valley can supply cut-to-length sleepers, or build the whole thing for you.

One 1.2 × 2.4 m sleeper bed grows enough salad for a family of four all summer. Add a second and you’re into courgettes, French beans, herbs, and kale from June through November. That’s a food system in 6 square metres.

Spring Growing Guide 2026

What to Grow
in Your New
Planter

You’ve built the bed. Now fill it with something that will actually feed you. Spring 2026 is the year to commit to growing your own — here’s what performs beautifully in a raised sleeper bed in Somerset.

Salad Leaves
Fastest return on effort. Sow now, cut in 4 weeks. Sow another row the week after. Succession-sowing means leaves all summer from a tiny area.
Climbing French Beans
Wait until after last frost — typically mid-May in the Frome valley. A hazel wigwam gives structure. High yield, deeply satisfying to pick fresh every evening.
Courgettes
One plant per bed is genuinely enough — it will fill the space by July. Raised beds suit them perfectly: warmth, drainage, and you can see what you’re harvesting.
Herbs: The Essentials
Chives, parsley, dill and coriander from seed now. Rosemary and thyme from cuttings. A permanent herb corner changes how you cook all year.
Kale & Chard
Sow now for summer-to-winter harvest. Cavolo Nero is the one — it produces over months, handles frost, and looks spectacular as a border plant.
Edible Flowers
Nasturtiums, borage, calendula at the edges. They attract pollinators, confuse pests, and you eat them in salads. Zero effort, maximum reward.

Essay · Philosophy

The Living
Material

Every piece of wood you work with was alive. Not metaphorically — literally alive, pulling water up through its rings, responding to drought years and flood years, laying down extra-dense heartwood in decades of stress. When you cut into a sleeper or a beam, you’re cutting into autobiography.

“Wood remembers the weather it grew in. Our job is to honour that memory.”

This is why Valley doesn’t rush. We let timber acclimatise in the workshop before we cut it. We read the grain before we reach for a blade. A knot isn’t a defect — it’s where a branch once reached toward light. The shake in old oak isn’t weakness — it’s the record of a storm it survived.

Working with wood well requires you to slow down. In a world that values speed above everything, that is a quietly radical act. The grain asks you to pay attention. The weight asks you to be honest about what the material wants to be.

Spring is the right time to think about this. The trees outside your workshop window are doing precisely what they’ve always done — waking up, pushing growth, reaching. They don’t rush. Neither should we.

Frome’s Quiet
Revolution

Frome doesn’t shout. That is its greatest trick. While other market towns hollowed out — charity shops replacing independents, chain coffee replacing local cafes — Frome quietly decided to become something else: a place where cooperative ownership, independent work, and community investment are the norm rather than the exception.

The Frome Independents movement gave local businesses a genuine collective identity. The Cheese & Grain kept the town’s gig economy alive without private investment. Edventure Frome turned empty buildings and spare land into workshops, studios, and growing spaces long before it was fashionable. The Repair Café started fixing things before the circular economy had a name.

Where Valley Fits In

Valley is part of that fabric. We source locally where we can — timber merchants who know where their wood comes from, tools bought second-hand from retiring craftspeople before we consider buying new. We work within the Frome network, the informal economy of favour and collaboration that runs quietly underneath the official business world.

Our workshop space exists because of community infrastructure that organisations like Edventure have kept alive. When we needed to expand our tool library, we borrowed before we bought. When we took on an apprentice last autumn, the onboarding happened partly through introductions from people we’d helped and been helped by over three years.

The Economics of Enough

Frome operates on a different economic logic to most places. Not growth for growth’s sake — enough. Enough to pay people fairly. Enough to maintain the space and the relationships. Enough to invest in the next project and take on the next person who needs a chance.

Valley shares that logic. We are not trying to scale to a national brand. We are trying to be genuinely useful to the place we live in, and to pass on the skills that make that possible. That is increasingly rare. That is exactly why it matters.

Valley uses AI-assisted design tools to plan every build before a blade touches wood. Not to replace craft — to serve it. When you can model a hundred joint variations in ten minutes, you arrive at the bench with better decisions already made.

The hand tool and the algorithm are not opposites. The algorithm handles optimisation — quantities, tolerances, carbon footprint, material efficiency. The hand decides what’s beautiful. Neither can do the other’s job.

Valley is building open pattern libraries for our most common project types — sleeper beds, cold frames, tool stores, garden structures — so clients can see precisely what they’re commissioning before we start. Transparent process, honest pricing, no surprises.

Community · Sustainability

The
Repair
Ethic

Frome’s Repair Café has fixed over 2,000 objects since it started — bikes, clothes, electronics, furniture. The point isn’t merely fixing things. It’s changing the relationship people have with their possessions. From consumer to custodian.

Valley shares that instinct. Before we build new, we ask: can this be repaired? Adapted? Preserved? Sometimes the most radical act is not making something new.

When Edventure Frome needed new workshop benches, we didn’t build from scratch. We salvaged old school workbenches, stripped them, re-topped them in solid oak, and fitted new vices. Total cost: 40% of new. Character: immeasurable. The benches now carry two histories instead of one.

Valley’s Three Principles
Repair First
Before building new, always ask whether the existing thing can be saved. Most of the time it can, with less effort than you expect.
Reclaim Second
Use salvaged, reclaimed, or surplus material before ordering virgin stock. The character is always better. The carbon footprint is always lower.
Build to Last
If we are making something new, we make it to outlast us. Proper joinery, proper timber, proper care. No flat-pack logic. No scheduled obsolescence.

Let Plants
Work
Together

Companion planting is one of the oldest tools a grower has. Pair the right plants and they protect each other, fix nitrogen, attract beneficials, and keep pests confused. Your sleeper bed is the perfect canvas.

The logic holds everywhere: diversity above ground mirrors diversity below. A monoculture is fragile. A mixed system is resilient. Same principle as the Frome independent economy. Same principle as Valley’s workshop.

Classic Pairings for a Sleeper Bed
Tomatoes+
BasilBasil repels aphids and whitefly. Both benefit — the basil grows better near tomatoes and the tomatoes taste better near basil.
Courgettes+
NasturtiumsNasturtiums act as a sacrificial trap crop — blackfly colonise them rather than the courgette stems. Also edible, also beautiful.
French Beans+
MarigoldsTagetes marigold roots exude a compound that deters soil nematodes. Beans fix nitrogen; marigolds protect it.
Kale+
DillDill attracts hoverflies and parasitic wasps that prey on cabbage whitefly and aphids. One dill plant does the work of a pesticide application.
Salad Leaves+
ChivesChives deter slugs — a genuine problem in Somerset raised beds. Both prefer similar growing conditions.
